MSc project · University of Galway

Do candidates think AI hiring is fair?

We looked at 155,000 recruitment experiences to see how candidates feel about being screened by AI, and what employers could do about it.

Major Business Analytics Project (MS5131) 2025 – 26 Team of 4

The question

More than three-quarters of large firms now use algorithmic tools somewhere in their hiring. But most of what we know about how candidates react comes from lab studies, usually with fewer than 500 people responding to invented scenarios. We wanted to see whether those findings held up on a much bigger dataset.

The data covered 155,000 recruitment experiences across ten industries and ten recruitment methods. For each one we had seven ratings from the candidate: fairness, transparency, algorithmic anxiety, withdrawal intent, employer brand, satisfaction, and whether they'd recommend the employer.

Headline numbers

155,000recruitment experiences, across 10 industries and 10 hiring methods.
89%chance that a candidate screened the traditional way rates the process as fairer than one screened by AI.
96.4%accuracy at predicting who was likely to withdraw. All three models landed here (AUC 0.972–0.974).

Fairness by recruitment method

What we tested

#HypothesisTestResult
H1AI-driven methods receive lower fairness scoresWelch's t-testd = −1.77supported
H2Transparency rises with perceived fairnessPearson correlationr = 0.704supported
H3Low fairness predicts high withdrawal intentPearson correlationr = −0.908supported
H4Fairness predicts employer brand perceptionPearson correlationr = 0.872supported

All four were significant at p < .001. For context, a Cohen's d of −1.77 is more than double the usual 0.80 cut-off for a "large" effect.

How we did it

Before touching the data, I turned the research question into the four testable hypotheses above and agreed the success metrics we'd judge each one by. That's the same job as writing acceptance criteria, just for a study instead of a system.

We started by just looking at the data: histograms, violin plots and confidence-interval charts. Then came the formal tests, which were Welch's t-test with Cohen's d, a one-way ANOVA across all ten methods, Pearson correlations and chi-square tests.

For the prediction side, we trained logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting models on eleven features with a stratified 80/20 split, and compared them on accuracy, F1 and AUC-ROC. They all performed about the same. The more interesting part was the random forest's feature importance, which put perceived fairness at the top as a predictor of withdrawal, ahead of employer brand and algorithmic anxiety.

The results went out as a stakeholder-ready report with charts written for people who run recruitment, not for statisticians.

What we'd tell employers

  • Keep a person in the loopUse AI for the first pass, but have people handle the evaluation, the communication and the final call.
  • Be upfront about itTell candidates when AI is involved and what it's doing, and give them a way to appeal.
  • Keep auditing for fairnessRun this kind of analysis regularly so you notice if candidate perceptions start to shift.
  • Start with fairnessIt was the strongest predictor of withdrawal, so that's where improvements pay off most.
  • Train the people running itHR teams who pick and run these tools need regular training on AI ethics.
  • Ask candidatesBuild a structured feedback loop on fairness and transparency at each stage.

BSc project · Andhra University

Online Education Management Tool

A platform a college could run on its own servers, so it could keep teaching online during COVID-19 without giving student data to an outside provider.

BSc final year, 2022 Role: Requirements & development, team of 7

The problem

When the pandemic hit, colleges in India had to move lectures online almost overnight. A lot of them ended up on third-party platforms, which meant handing over student and staff data. Our idea was to build something a college could host itself, so the data stayed in-house and the costs stayed low.

What it does

  • Separate logins and dashboards for admins, faculty and students
  • Students upload assignments directly to the right lecturer
  • Faculty enter marks, attendance and results
  • A chat so students can message lecturers with questions
  • Notices that go out to students and staff
  • Online tests with randomised questions, several levels and cut-off marks

How we built it

  1. RequirementsI gathered requirements, documented the user flows and wireframed the screens. Then we wrote the SRS: ten functional requirements, the non-functional ones, and how we'd validate each.
  2. DesignDesigned a 12-table MySQL schema and broke the system into modules, with acceptance criteria agreed with the developers.
  3. BuildA Java 11 desktop app built with Swing, talking to MySQL over JDBC.
  4. TestUnit, integration, system and user acceptance testing, all checked against the SRS.
